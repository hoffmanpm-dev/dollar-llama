You Are Always the Highest Bidder

August 2026

If you have ever bought a house, you know this feeling.

You went back and forth on the number, landed on something that felt like a stretch, and sent it over braced for the counter. It never came. They just said yes.

For half a second that feels wonderful. Then your stomach turns over and a very clear thought arrives.

I should have offered less.

That thought has bothered me for years, because I have it every time. We buy small rental properties here in St. Louis, usually ones that have sat for months or need a roof. We offer well below asking. And still, when the seller signs, my first reaction is not satisfaction. It is that I left money on the table.

Most of our money is not in buildings. It is in index funds and bonds, the same as most people's. But buildings are the only place I can watch the bidding happen, and watching it changed how I think about everything else we own.

The feeling is correct. It is just badly understood.

An accepted offer tells you one thing. Your number was high enough. It never tells you how much higher than it needed to be. Maybe five thousand dollars. Maybe fifty. Nobody tells you what the seller would have taken.

And whether there were six competing offers or none at all, you were the highest bidder. If that house sat for eight months, every other buyer in your city saw it and passed. When the seller signed, you were the only person willing to pay that much for it that day.

That is what buying is. A house, a stock, a bond, a painting at an estate sale. You are always the one who was willing to pay the most, and somebody else always looked at the same thing at the same price and said no. Economists call it the winner's curse. You do not need the name. You just need to notice that every time you buy anything, the rest of the market voted against you. How much that should bother you depends entirely on where you are standing.

In the Stock Market, All You Have Is Patience

When I buy shares of a big company, I ask what I know that the person selling to me does not. Realistically, nothing. I read the same public filings. There are people whose entire job is studying that one company, and computers reacting to news in less than a second. Whatever thought I have had, several thousand people had before lunch.

So knowing more is out. Thinking harder is out.

You may be thinking about Warren Buffett right about now, and that is fair. He spent a lifetime proving that reading the same public facts more carefully than everybody else can genuinely work. I am not Warren Buffett. You are probably not either. He will come back later, because he has something useful for the rest of us. But a handful of people beating this market over a lifetime is not a plan for your money.

Plenty of people will happily take your money and try it for you. The record is not on their side. Stretch the measurement out far enough and there is essentially no category where most professional managers beat their index. I have written about that argument before.

What is left is behavior, and it is not nothing. Most people end up with less than the funds they own actually returned, because they buy after prices go up and sell after they go down. Sitting still is a genuine advantage, available to anyone, and almost nobody uses it.

That sounds like bad news. It is actually the reassurance. With that many people arguing over the price all day, the number on the screen is what a huge crowd settled on, not one stranger's guess. You are paying about what everybody thinks it is worth, and you can leave whenever you like at roughly that number.

The trouble starts when someone buys in a market like that and believes they found a bargain.

In June, SpaceX sold shares to the public at $135 and raised about $75 billion, the biggest stock sale in history. They closed the first day at $161, nineteen percent higher. By the beginning of August they were down to $105. As I write this in late August they are back around $138.

The company had raised private money for twenty years at nearly any price it wanted, and chose this summer to go public. Companies do that when selling stock is their cheapest way to raise money, which is another way of saying the moment buyers are paying the most.

Anyone who bought at $161 that first day paid nineteen percent more than the company itself had just agreed to accept. Two and a half months later they are down about fourteen percent, and at the August low they were down closer to thirty-five. SpaceX got its $135 either way. But those buyers found out fast. The market told them, publicly, in numbers, for free. That is unpleasant, and it is also a service. I wrote about picking single companies when SpaceX first filed.

Where the Gap Can Be Enormous

Your house is the opposite kind of market, and probably the largest thing you own. No crowd is arguing over the price of a three bedroom on your block, which cuts both ways. Knowing things is worth real money here, which is why people make a living at it. It is also why the mistakes are large and quiet. If you paid fifteen percent too much, nothing tells you tomorrow, and you may not find out for a decade.

So the question worth asking, and almost nobody asks it, is why everyone who passed decided to pass. Sometimes that is a real warning. If a house sat because the foundation is failing and every contractor in town knows it, you want to know before you become the one person who said yes.

Usually it is nothing. The serious operators were never looking at that listing. Large firms find their own deals by sending letters to owners who never listed, making calls, knocking on doors, all so they never compete on a public listing. By the time a property is sitting there with photographs and an asking price, it is not their market. If a professional studied it and walked away, be nervous. If a professional never looked, that tells you nothing at all.

What Is It Worth to You?

Here is the part that took me longest to accept, and it answers that queasy feeling.

When there is no crowd setting the price, you have to set it yourself. All you have is your own arithmetic about what the thing produces, or what it will do for your life. If the numbers still work at the price you paid, then the price you paid was fine.

I have talked myself out of deals chasing the last ten thousand dollars, and I have never once been glad about it. Paying a little too much turns a great investment into a good one. Losing it over pride turns it into no investment at all, and no investment returns nothing.

I said Buffett would come back. He does not buy a stock because he expects somebody to pay more for it later. He works out what a business is worth from the cash it will produce over its lifetime, and buys when the price sits below that. Price is what you pay. Value is what you get. Only one of those is set by other people.

That is exactly what we do on a building. Not what the next bidder would have paid, but what it earns and whether the price makes sense against that. We look for about twenty percent a year on the cash we put in, and a large share of that comes from work nobody pays us for. Katie handles the day to day. We make decisions on a Saturday morning that a firm would pay a salary to make on a Tuesday. That is a real advantage. It is also a job.

The awkward part is that Buffett does this in the very market where I just said you have no advantage. He is the exception and nobody knows it better than he does. In his 2013 letter to shareholders he laid out the instructions for his own wife's inheritance. Ninety percent into a low cost S&P 500 index fund, ten percent into short term government bonds.

So take the thinking, not the trade. Ask what something is worth to you based on what it produces or gives you. In the stock market you will almost always decide the price is about right and buy the index fund. In a market with four buyers and no screen, you will sometimes decide it is worth considerably more than the asking price.

Same question. Different answers, because the markets are different.

The Test I Run on Myself

Being the highest bidder is unavoidable. Refuse on principle and you own nothing, which is its own expensive mistake.

But think about the last time you bought an index fund. Did you feel that flicker of regret? Did any part of you think, I should have paid less?

I never have. Not once. Not because index funds are magic, but because the price was set by millions of people out in the open, I could see it, and I knew I could sell tomorrow for roughly the same number. There was nothing to second-guess.

The regret only arrives when I have won something. I have learned to treat it as information about the market rather than instruction about the deal. It is my own mind telling me I paid a price nobody else would pay, somewhere I will not find out for years whether that was smart. Sometimes it was.

But the feeling is never wrong about what it is reporting. You were the highest bidder. The only question left is whether you can say what it is worth to you, and why everyone else stopped.
